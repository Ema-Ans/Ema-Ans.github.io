Multilingual Visualization Interpretation

When a chart crosses a language boundary, what exactly has to travel with it?

I study how people interpret, explain, and recreate visualizations across linguistic and cultural contexts. Rather than treating localization as text translation alone, this work examines how people reason about notation, layout, visual convention, and what should change when a chart is adapted for another audience.

from encounter to redesign

How the Study Works

The study follows what happens as a person moves from simply encountering an unfamiliar chart to communicating and ultimately redesigning it for someone else.

  1. 01

    Interpret

    What does this chart say?

    Participants first make sense of an unfamiliar visualization and its data, labels, notation, and conventions.

  2. 02

    Explain

    How would you communicate it?

    They explain the chart for another audience, revealing what information they preserve, omit, translate, or reinterpret.

  3. 03

    Recreate

    What would you change?

    They adapt the chart for a new audience, making explicit decisions about language, notation, layout, and visual semantics.

contribution ledger

What I Built

01

Multi-stage study protocol

Designed a participant-study structure that separates interpretation, explanation, and recreation rather than treating visualization understanding as a single task.

02

Task design

Developed chart tasks that expose different kinds of locale-dependent reasoning while still allowing participants to work naturally with external tools.

03

Human coding framework

Built a structured coding scheme for participant responses, awareness of locale-specific conventions, and tool-use behavior.

04

Component-level evaluation

Designed evaluation criteria for participant-created visualizations so that language, notation, visual convention, fidelity, and completeness can be assessed separately rather than collapsed into one accuracy score.

05

Analysis framework

Structured the study so that participant behavior, produced artifacts, tool use, and workload can be analyzed together.

the interaction question

Why This Is an HCI Problem

Visualization localization is usually framed as a translation problem.

But people interact with charts through learned conventions: how numbers are grouped, where axes appear, what colors imply, which direction information is read, and which tools they trust when something feels unfamiliar.

This project studies that interaction between people, visual conventions, and the tools used to bridge unfamiliarity.

The broader design question is not simply “can we translate this chart?” but: what should a visualization system preserve, explain, adapt, or redesign when meaning depends on locale?

working premise

Localization is not only translation. It is a design decision about what meaning needs to travel.
