Speech / Audio LLM Pretraining

Learning how research is actually done, one objective and ablation at a time.

How do we make speech pretraining more efficient without stripping away the structure that makes speech meaningful?

I joined Bhiksha Raj’s lab as an undergraduate, which at the time felt a bit like being quietly dropped into a room where everyone was thinking five papers ahead of me and casually speaking in objectives, ablations, and loss functions.

Under his guidance, I explored resource-efficient pretraining strategies for speech and audio language models through compression-based representations and synthetic supervision for speech error modeling.

Also the lab where, as an undergrad, I became convinced some people naturally speak in loss functions.

what I worked on

From experimental idea to senior honors thesis

I prototyped compression-based representations using deep compression autoencoders as a possible route toward lighter-weight pretraining signals for speech and audio models.

I also built synthetic supervision pipelines to model speech errors at scale, with the aim of creating richer training signals without relying entirely on large quantities of manually labeled data.

Across the thesis, I worked on model prototyping, experimental design, analysis, and research writing.

apprenticeship, really

The teaching that happened in the margins

A huge part of this project was mentorship. Bhiksha Raj took me into the lab as an undergraduate and taught me what it looks like to think rigorously, argue carefully, and read research with patience.

Some of my clearest memories from this period are of him walking me through papers paragraph by paragraph, explaining not just what a method did, but why the authors had framed the problem that way in the first place. Later, that mentorship continued through Zoom calls and long conversations that remained just as precise, generous, and formative.

maybe this is where the professor dream really started: being taught with patience, in all the places no one else sees.

why this still matters to me

More compute and more data are not the only path forward

This project shaped the way I think about machine learning systems more broadly: not just in terms of accuracy, but in terms of efficiency, scalability, and what kinds of signal are actually worth preserving.

It also pushed me toward a recurring question in my later work: what happens when we stop treating scale as the entire answer and instead ask what structure really matters?
