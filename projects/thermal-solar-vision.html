FIRST RESEARCH PROJECT · COMPUTER VISION · IEEE VCIP 2024

Thermal Vision for Solar Panels

Sophomore me, a lot of Jupyter notebooks, and one surprisingly difficult vision problem.

How do you make computer vision behave on close-range thermal images?

This was one of my earliest research projects, and the one that taught me how research feels in practice: collecting imperfect data, debugging notebook pipelines, comparing methods carefully, and discovering that preprocessing decisions can change everything.

Working with Eduardo Feo-Flushing’s group, I studied classical feature detectors and descriptors on close-range thermal images of solar panels captured under real-world variation.

The project where I learned that “just run OpenCV” is not, in fact, a research method.

baby researcher lore

Armed with curiosity and too many notebook cells

Huge thank you to Eduardo Feo-Flushing for taking me in early, teaching me how to think through computer- vision experiments properly, and tolerating an enthusiastic tiny researcher who wanted to poke every step of the pipeline to see what happened.

what we were trying to figure out

Which classical methods survive messy thermal imagery?

Most feature-method evaluations focus on visible-light images or cleaner benchmark settings. We studied a more specific case: close-range thermal imagery of solar panels, where temperature gradients, shadows, camera conditions, and color mapping affect what algorithms can detect and match.

We built a dataset using a FLIR Duo Pro R camera and evaluated classical detectors and descriptors under changes in time of day, camera angle, rotation, motion blur, and colormap choice.

what we found

The algorithm was only part of the story

best detectorAKAZEmost reliable across the study
best descriptorSURFstrongest overall robustness
best colormapsJET / RAINBOWhigh-contrast mappings performed best

The broader lesson was simple but important: in thermal imaging, performance is not only about the method. Normalization, preprocessing, and even how temperature is mapped to color can change what the system “sees.”

Preprocessing can absolutely bully your results.

why this still matters to me

My introduction to careful empirical vision research

This project taught me to treat datasets as constructed objects rather than neutral inputs, to compare methods systematically, and to notice how seemingly small preprocessing choices alter downstream behavior.

A lot of my later work—in multimodal behavior analysis, visualization, and human-centered AI—still carries that lesson forward: before interpreting a system’s output, look closely at how the data was captured, transformed, and made legible in the first place.
