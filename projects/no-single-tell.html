No Single Tell

Visible behavior during extended decision-making in elite chess.

How much of thinking can we actually see?

I study how gaze, posture, self-touch, and other visible behaviors unfold while expert chess players make difficult decisions. The project combines long-form broadcast video with temporally aligned game state, clocks, and Stockfish-derived decision context.

The short version: there is no magical “tell.” Behavior becomes interesting when you stop treating it as one.

what I built

A human-centered measurement workflow

I designed the behavioral measurement workflow end-to-end: a temporal coding taxonomy for visible behavior, custom interfaces for human annotation and multi-reviewer adjudication, synchronized video–game alignment tools, and analysis pipelines linking behavioral events to decision context.

The annotation workflow lets reviewers scrub tightly around a decision episode and independently code posture, gaze, self-touch, chess interaction, and visibility over time. I also built the process used to compare and resolve annotations across coders.

hci angle

The measurement question underneath the model

Rather than asking whether a model can infer an internal state from a face or pose, this work asks a more basic measurement question: when humans interpret visible behavior as evidence of thinking, what can those observations actually support?

The project treats behavior as situated, temporally evolving, and person-dependent—and studies the measurement process itself, including what is observable, how humans code it, and when those observations become meaningful in context.
