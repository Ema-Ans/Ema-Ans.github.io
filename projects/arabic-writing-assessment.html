NATIONAL RESEARCH FUND–SUPPORTED RESEARCH · 2025

Arabic Writing Assessment

Can a model learn writing quality rather than memorizing the question?

As part of a National Research Fund–supported collaboration between Carnegie Mellon and Qatar University, I worked on automated Arabic writing assessment in low-resource settings.

My focus was cross-prompt generalization: training on writing from several prompts, then evaluating whether the model could score essays responding to a prompt it had never seen before.

the problem

Scoring the Writing, Not the Prompt

Most automated essay-scoring systems perform best when training and testing happen on the same writing prompt. That is convenient experimentally, but awkward in real educational settings where new questions appear all the time.

We asked whether Arabic writing quality could be modeled across prompts rather than rebuilding a scoring system every time the question changed.

a side experiment

Can Synthetic Data Help Without Becoming the Dataset?

We also experimented with cross-lingual augmentation: English learner essays were translated into Arabic with prompts designed to preserve learner-like errors rather than silently “fix” the writing.

The useful question was not simply whether more data helped, but when synthetic data complemented — or distorted — the original scoring signal.

translation models have an unfortunate habit of correcting the mistakes you specifically asked them to preserve

trajectory

Why I Still Care About This

Automated assessment is ultimately a model standing in for a human judgment.

This project was one of the first places I became interested in what happens when a model leaves the clean setting it was trained in: new prompts, sparse data, different rubrics, and linguistic structures that were never designed around English.

That question — whether a system remains meaningful when context changes — followed me into much of my later work.
