Prompt-Agnostic Arabic Writing Assessment

Generalizing writing-quality judgments beyond familiar prompts.

Can a model learn something about writing quality without memorizing the prompt?

As part of a Qatar National Research Fund–supported collaboration between Carnegie Mellon University Qatar and Qatar University, I worked on automated Arabic writing assessment in low-resource settings.

My focus was cross-prompt generalization: learning from essays written for several prompts, then scoring writing for a prompt the model had never seen during training.

The annoying part: essays change. Prompts change. Rubrics change. The model still has to behave.

the problem

Train on familiar prompts. Evaluate on a completely unseen one.

Can we teach the model writing quality, rather than one writing prompt at a time?

what I worked on

Models, language features, and reproducible evaluation

I built and evaluated two complementary approaches to prompt-agnostic Arabic writing assessment. PAIR-Net asks whether essays can be compared relative to one another across prompts; ALiF-Net asks how much can be recovered from interpretable Arabic-specific linguistic evidence.

I also developed the cross-prompt evaluation pipeline, reproducible training setup, representation cache, and augmentation experiments used to test both approaches.

why I still care about this project

A model is standing in for human judgment

Automated assessment is ultimately a model standing in for a human judgment. That made generalization more interesting to me than maximizing a score on one familiar prompt.

A useful assessment system has to survive new questions, different rubrics, sparse training data, and the linguistic structure of the people whose writing it evaluates. This was one of the first places I became interested in the gap between benchmark performance and reliable behavior when context changes.
