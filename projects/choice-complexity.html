Choice Complexity and Decision-Making in Chess

What makes a decision hard when several options are all annoyingly reasonable?

We study whether decision difficulty can be understood not only from the position itself, but from the structure of the available choice set. Using engine-evaluated candidate moves, we build computational measures of choice complexity and relate them to how long people deliberate and the quality of the decision they eventually make.

the model

The Core Idea

Difficulty can live in the structure of the choice set.

A position with one clearly dominant move presents a very different decision environment from one where several alternatives remain similarly viable.

We represent this difference using entropy-inspired measures over engine-evaluated candidate moves. A concentrated distribution corresponds to a choice set dominated by one alternative; a more dispersed distribution reflects several competitive options.

Entropy is not intended as a direct measure of subjective difficulty. It gives us a computational description of the decision environment that exists before the human choice is made.

the central claim

Difficulty is not only a property of the person. It can also be a property of the choice set.

from alternatives to outcomes

We begin with the alternatives available in a position and progressively turn them into a representation of the decision environment.

the interaction question

Why This Is an HCI Question

Interactive systems often infer that a person is struggling from what happens after a choice: longer response time, lower confidence, more errors, or poorer outcomes.

But difficulty can also come from the structure of the options the system presents.

This project asks whether we can describe that decision environment computationally and use it to better interpret human deliberation. Chess is useful here because the available alternatives can be evaluated at scale while the human decision itself remains observable.

The broader question extends beyond chess: how should systems represent choice complexity when trying to understand, support, or adapt to human decision-making?

public takeaway

What We’re Learning

A single difficulty score does not fully describe the decision environment.

Positions with one dominant option and positions with several competitive options can look very different from the perspective of a human decision-maker, even when a conventional engine summary makes them appear similarly difficult.

Our ongoing analyses examine whether measures of broader choice structure help explain variation in deliberation time and decision quality beyond simpler descriptions of the position.

Detailed model comparisons, effect sizes, and final statistical results are staying with the manuscript while the analysis is being finalized.
