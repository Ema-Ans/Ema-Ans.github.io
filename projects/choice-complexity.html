Choice Complexity

Decision-making in chess when several options are all plausible.

What makes a decision difficult when several options are all plausible?

Rather than treating difficulty as a single engine score or only the gap between the best alternatives, we study the structure of the available choice set. Using engine evaluations across candidate moves, we develop computational measures of choice complexity and ask how they relate to decision time and the quality of the decision ultimately made.

Because “there are too many annoyingly reasonable moves here” is, unfortunately, not yet a scientific metric.

the idea

Difficulty can live in the shape of the choice set.

A chess position does not need one obviously bad choice to be difficult. Sometimes the difficulty comes from having several alternatives that remain similarly viable.

We use entropy-inspired measures over engine-evaluated candidate moves to represent how dispersed or concentrated the choice landscape is. A position with one clearly dominant move has a different structure from one in which many alternatives remain competitive.

The goal is not to claim that entropy measures subjective experience directly. It gives us a computational language for describing the decision environment before a human choice is made.

why this is hci

Representing the decision environment itself

Interactive systems often infer that a user is struggling from outcomes such as response time, errors, or confidence. But difficulty can also be a property of the choices the system presents.

This project asks how the structure of an option space can be represented computationally, and whether those representations help explain how long a person deliberates and how well they decide. Chess provides a rich setting because alternatives can be evaluated at scale while the human decision remains observable.

what we are finding

A choice landscape carries information that one score cannot.

Decision time is not explained by position difficulty in one simple way. Positions with several competitive alternatives create a different decision environment from positions with one clearly dominant move.

Our analyses suggest that measures of broader choice structure can reveal patterns missed when difficulty is reduced to a single evaluation score. Detailed models, comparisons, and effect sizes will remain with the manuscript while it is being prepared.
